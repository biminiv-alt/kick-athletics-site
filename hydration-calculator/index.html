    The easy check
    • Pale yellow pee on game morning means they are on track. Dark yellow means they are behind, so start sipping now.
    • Fill a marked bottle the night before. If most of it isn't gone by warmups, they are behind.
    • Thirst, a headache or feeling "foggy" in the second half are early signs they need more.

    Dizziness, confusion, vomiting, or a kid who stops sweating in the heat: stop play, cool them down and get medical help right away. Don't try to fix that with more water.

    How the calculator works

    The numbers come from widely published guidance for healthy, active kids, rounded to amounts you can actually measure with a water bottle. They are conservative on purpose.

    • Whole day: about two-thirds of an ounce of fluid per pound of body weight, and about three-quarters of an ounce per pound when it's hot or the game runs long. Milk, fruit, soup and yogurt count toward it.
    • Before: most of the drinking should happen before the game. A bottle about 2 hours ahead, then a few sips before warmups.
    • During: a few big sips every 15 to 20 minutes of hard play. Bigger kids, harder sports and hotter days sit at the top of the range.
    • After: refill the bottle and finish it in the first hour, alongside a real snack or meal.

    If you want to be exact, weigh your athlete before and after a hard practice. Each pound lost is roughly 16 to 24 oz of fluid to replace over the next couple of hours.

    Common questions

    Can kids drink too much water? It's rare, but yes. Drinking far more than they sweat, especially plain water over a long, hot day, can lower the salt in their blood. That's why the plan has a top limit, and why salty snacks and a sports drink matter on long tournament days. If pee is clear all day, ease off.

    What about cramps? Late-game cramping is often more about salt than water. Read why cramping is a sodium gap.

    Energy drinks? No. Caffeine and energy drinks aren't recommended for kids and teens, before games or any other time.

    General guidance for healthy kids, not medical advice. Every athlete sweats differently. If your child has a medical condition, a heart or kidney concern, or takes medication, ask their pediatrician what's right for them.

How much protein does a youth athlete actually need?

Protein is the nutrient parents worry about most and get wrong in both directions. Some kids barely get any outside of dinner. Others are being handed protein shakes they do not need. Here is what a growing athlete actually needs, in plain numbers.

The quick answer

Active youth athletes generally do well on roughly 0.6 to 0.7 grams of protein per pound of body weight per day. That is noticeably more than a non-athlete their age needs, but far less than the bodybuilding numbers you see online.

  • 80 lb athlete: about 50 to 55 g a day
  • 100 lb athlete: about 65 to 70 g a day
  • 120 lb athlete: about 75 to 85 g a day
  • 140 lb athlete: about 90 to 100 g a day

Older teens in heavy training sit toward the top of each range. Younger kids in a lighter season sit toward the bottom.

Spread it out, do not stack it at dinner

The most common pattern is a tiny breakfast, a light lunch, and a big plate of chicken at night. The total might look fine, but the body uses protein better when it arrives in steady doses. Aim for something like 15 to 25 grams at each meal and a little in snacks.

What that looks like in real food:

  • Two eggs: about 12 g
  • A cup of Greek yogurt: about 15 to 20 g
  • A glass of milk: about 8 g
  • A turkey sandwich: about 20 g
  • A palm-sized piece of chicken: about 25 to 30 g
  • Two tablespoons of peanut butter: about 7 g
  • A cheese stick: about 7 g

Breakfast is where most kids fall short. If yours eats cereal and runs, start there. Here is the game-day breakfast that fixes it.

Does my kid need protein powder?

Usually not. Most youth athletes can hit their number with normal food once breakfast and snacks are fixed. A shake can be a handy tool for a picky eater or a kid who cannot face food after a late practice, but it is a convenience, not a requirement. Skip anything sold as a "pre-workout" or with added stimulants.

Protein on game day

On game day, protein plays a supporting role. Carbs are the fuel for the game itself. Protein at breakfast helps steady energy and keeps them full, and protein after the game helps recovery. A big, heavy, protein-loaded meal right before playing is a recipe for a sluggish first half. For what the whole plate should look like, see what a game-day plate actually looks like at 12.

Recovery: the window that matters

After a game or hard practice, get some protein and carbs in within an hour or so. Chocolate milk, a yogurt with fruit, or a turkey wrap all work. At tournaments this matters even more, because game 2 starts with whatever game 1 left behind.

This week

Write down what your athlete eats on one normal school day and add up the protein with the list above. If breakfast and lunch together come in under about a third of their number, that is the gap to fix first.

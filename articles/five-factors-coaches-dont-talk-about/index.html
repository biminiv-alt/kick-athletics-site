THE FUNDAMENTALS

The five factors coaches don't talk about

By Kick Athletics · September 16, 2026 · 5 min read

Your athlete works hard. Practices come and go. Games come and go. And somewhere in the middle of a season you look up and realize that some of the other kids on the roster keep pulling ahead and yours does not.

It is easy to blame the coach. Or genetics. Or bad luck at position selection. Sometimes those are the answer. But often the real gap is not in the practice or the games at all. It is in the 22 hours a day that happen around them.

Sports scientists who study youth performance keep landing on the same five inputs. None of them are talent. All of them are fully in your control at home. Here they are, in the order they usually show up as the missing piece.

1. Nutrition

Youth athletes 9 to 15 need real food at specific times. Cereal-only breakfast on a game day is the most common breakfast mistake in youth sports and it is why so many kids fade in the second half. A plate that gets a palm-sized protein, a fist of carbs, and one piece of fruit, eaten 2 to 3 hours before the whistle, changes everything.

The second most common mistake is tournament food. Concession stands sell things that make money for the tournament, not things that fuel athletes. Pretzels and Gatorade between game 1 and game 2 is why so many kids look flat in game 3.

2. Hydration

Most youth athletes are chronically underhydrated. Even a 1 to 2 percent drop in body water shows up as slower reaction times, more mistakes late in games, and worse decision making. Chronically underhydrated kids sleep worse and recover slower.

The fix is boring. A 32-ounce water bottle refilled three times a day (before school, after school, and during dinner) closes 80 percent of the gap. Most families do not do this because it seems too simple to matter. It matters more than any recovery product on the market.

3. Sleep

Youth athletes 10 to 15 need 9 to 11 hours of sleep to fully recover from training. Most get 7 to 8 on a good night. Two weeks of that adds up to a sleep debt they cannot out-train, and it usually shows up as fading late in games, poor mood on the drive home, and slower recovery between practices.

Consistent bedtime matters more than absolute hours. A 9:30 bedtime hit within 30 minutes every night beats a 9:00 target that varies between 8:15 and 10:45. Weekends included. Phone charging outside the bedroom.

4. Recovery

The 24 hours after a hard game is when the body actually adapts. Skipping recovery is why some kids look stronger by mid-season and others look burned out. Real food within 60 minutes of the final whistle. Water instead of soda. Early night. No second training session the next day.

Recovery is the input most families skip because it does not look like effort. But the kids who recover well practice harder next week. That is where the compounding happens.

5. Game routine

Elite performers build the exact same pregame ritual every time. Same meal timing. Same warmup order. Same mental cue right before the whistle. Youth athletes without a routine show up as a different player every game. It is quiet, but coaches notice.

You do not need a complicated routine. Meal 3 hours before. Leave the house 90 minutes before. Warm up starts 45 minutes before. One mental cue 5 minutes before. Same order every time. Consistency beats intensity every time.
